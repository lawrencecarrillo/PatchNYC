02 — Road conditions

What's out there

High risk
Deep potholes
Can cause sudden front wheel loss and ejection from the bike. Most dangerous when filled with water and depth is invisible. Common after freeze-thaw cycles in late winter and early spring.
High risk
Raised manhole covers
Especially dangerous at speed or at an angle. A raised cover can catch a tire and pull the front wheel sideways. NYC has over 300,000 manholes — many sit well above or below grade.
Medium risk
Uneven pavement / tree roots
Raised edges from tree root expansion create lip hazards that can catch narrow tires. Common in Brooklyn, Queens, and tree-lined avenues in Manhattan.
Medium risk
Wet metal surfaces
Grates, manhole covers, and painted lane markings become extremely slippery when wet. Brake and turn before you reach them, not on them.
Medium risk
Debris & broken glass
Broken glass and metal shards are frequent near construction zones. Flat tires from debris are the #1 reason cyclists end up stopped in traffic lanes — a secondary hazard.
Lower risk
Faded / missing lane markings
Worn bike lane paint can make lanes invisible at night or in rain. Drivers may not know they're sharing space. Report missing signage so DOT can prioritize repainting.
03 — Hand signals

Signal before you move

NYC law requires cyclists to signal turns. Signaling is also your clearest tool for preventing dooring, rear-end collisions, and intersection conflicts. Use these every time.

Left turn
Extend your left arm straight out, parallel to the ground. Hold for at least 3 seconds before turning. Make eye contact with drivers behind you when possible.
Right turn
Extend your left arm up at a 90° angle (elbow bent, hand pointing up). Alternatively, extend your right arm straight out — both are legally accepted in NYC.
Stop / slowing
Extend your left arm down at a 45° angle, palm facing back. Use this before any sudden deceleration — especially important on shared paths with cyclists close behind.
Hazard ahead
Point down at the road toward the hazard — left hand for left-side hazards, right hand for right-side ones. This is informal but universally understood by cyclists and essential on group rides.
04 — Safety tips

Ride smarter

01
Wear a helmet
NYC does not legally require helmets for adult cyclists, but head injuries are the leading cause of cyclist fatalities. A properly fitted helmet reduces fatal head injury risk by up to 65%. If you ride Citibike daily — get your own helmet.
02
Ride with traffic, not against it
Wrong-way riding is illegal in NYC and dramatically increases collision risk. Drivers and pedestrians don't expect cyclists coming from the wrong direction, and reaction time drops to near zero.
03
Be visible at night
NYC law requires a white front light and red rear reflector after dark. Evening hours (6pm–midnight) account for the highest share of cyclist deaths. Add a rear blinker — it's the single most effective visibility upgrade you can make.
04
The door zone is a lane
Stay at least 4 feet from parked cars to avoid being doored. Dooring accounts for up to 20% of reported cyclist injuries in NYC. Watch for occupied vehicles, interior lights, and exhaust — signs a door may open.
05
Take the lane when you need it
When a bike lane is blocked by a delivery truck, construction, or double-parked car, you have the legal right to take the vehicle lane. Signal, check over your shoulder, and merge decisively — hesitation is more dangerous than action.
06
Watch for turning trucks
Large vehicles turning right from the left side of a bike lane are the cause of the most deadly cyclist-vehicle collisions in NYC. Never pass a large vehicle on the right when it could be turning. If you're at a light next to a truck, wait behind it.
07
Scan intersections before crossing
Even on a green light, scan left-right-left before entering an intersection. Distracted driving caused 1,544 cyclist injuries in 2024. A green light means you have the right of way — it doesn't mean every car is stopping.
08
Report hazards — don't swerve
A sudden swerve around a pothole can put you in traffic. Slow down before hazards, not on them. Then report it on PatchNYC or 311 so the next rider isn't caught off guard.
05 — Common courtesy

Share the road

NYC bike infrastructure is shared with millions of people every day. These aren't laws — they're the unwritten code that keeps everyone moving safely and without conflict.

→
Announce when passing
Say "on your left" clearly before overtaking another cyclist or pedestrian on a shared path. Don't blow past silently — a startled person can veer directly into your path.
→
Don't block the lane stopped at lights
If you're slower than the other cyclists at a light, position yourself to the right or behind faster riders. Blocking forces faster cyclists into traffic to pass you when the light changes.
→
One ear open, always
Keep at least one earbud out while riding in the city. You need to hear car horns, emergency vehicles, and other cyclists calling out. NYC law prohibits headphones covering both ears while cycling.
→
Yield to pedestrians in the crosswalk
Pedestrians always have the right of way in a crosswalk, even if you have a green light. Slow down before marked crossings at busy intersections — don't rely on pedestrians to jump out of the way.
→
Don't ride on the sidewalk
Sidewalk riding is illegal in NYC for cyclists 14 and older and is one of the most common sources of conflict between cyclists and pedestrians. If there's no bike lane, ride in the street.
→
Point out hazards for the rider behind you
Use the hazard point signal when you spot a pothole, grate, or debris ahead. The rider behind you has less time to react than you did. This is the simplest act of community you can do on a bike.
→
Lock up properly and fully
Lock both your frame and wheel to a fixed structure. An improperly locked bike blocking a ramp, curb cut, or fire hydrant creates accessibility hazards for others. Use designated bike corrals when available.
